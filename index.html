Dossier — November 2026

The digital euro is not a harmless payment upgrade.

The EU is negotiating a central bank digital currency. The ECB says it will complement cash; critics say it creates a programmable, surveillable and fragile monetary layer. This dossier separates what is law, what is technical preparation and what remains a political choice.

01 What is actually happening, in three points

1 — Nothing is law yet

The Commission proposed the regulation in 2023, the Council fixed its position in December 2025 and Parliament confirmed its mandate in July 2026. Trilogue negotiations are still running in November 2026.

2 — The ECB is preparing anyway

The preparation phase ended in October 2025. The ECB continues technical work, selected 36 payment service providers for a 12-month pilot planned for the second half of 2027, and says issuance could be considered in 2029 only after the regulation is adopted.

3 — The risk is architectural

Even with privacy safeguards, a retail CBDC concentrates payment data, holding limits and future programmability inside infrastructure controlled by public and private gatekeepers.

Legal status — updated November 2026

One project, three clocks

The digital euro is moving on three separate timelines: legislation, technical preparation and monetary-policy decision. Conflating them is how “it is already decided” myths spread.

A What is decided vs what is open

Already fixed

Positions adopted

Commission proposal, Council mandate, Parliament mandate, ECB preparation work and pilot selection.

Still negotiated

Trilogue in progress

Final holding limit, merchant fees, sanctions clauses, wallet distribution details, enforcement and rollout safeguards.

B How the machine moves

Under the ordinary legislative procedure, the Commission proposes, Parliament and Council adopt positions, then negotiate in trilogue. The ECB cannot issue the digital euro before the regulation exists, but it can prepare infrastructure, rules and pilot services so that “technical readiness” becomes political pressure for adoption.

C Promises vs criticisms

What institutions promise

A cash complement, free basic services, offline cash-like privacy, no remuneration, holding limits to protect banks and no direct Eurosystem identification of users.

What critics answer

Centralised ledgers still create metadata and fraud risks; payment providers remain identifiable gatekeepers; future programmability can be added by technical standards; mandatory acceptance can disadvantage better private rails; and “complement” does not prevent cash displacement.

D Timeline (2021 – 2029)

So… is the digital euro already launched?

No. There is no legal-tender digital euro in circulation. The pilot is preparation; the 2029 date is conditional on adoption of the regulation and a later ECB decision. The real fight is over the final legal text.

Technical dossier

How the proposed CBDC would actually work

The digital euro would not be “just another payment app”. It is a retail claim on the Eurosystem distributed through supervised payment providers, with online and offline modes, holding limits and a waterfall link to bank accounts.

01 Architecture in plain language

Users would hold digital euros in wallets provided by banks or payment service providers. Online payments would settle through Eurosystem infrastructure; offline payments would use pre-funded devices. A linked bank account could automatically top up payments above the holding limit — convenient, but also a permanent pipe between central bank money and commercial bank surveillance.

02 Three design choices, three failure modes

Central ledger, pseudonymised users

The ECB says it would not identify individuals, but transaction records would still exist inside a central infrastructure. Pseudonymisation is not anonymity; metadata can reveal behaviour, and providers remain legally able to identify users for AML checks.

Offline mode and fraud recourse

Offline payments promise cash-like privacy, but if a device is lost, hacked or used fraudulently, the absence of central transaction history complicates dispute resolution. Security depends on hardware, user custody and rules still being written.

Programmability by standards

The current proposal says the digital euro would not be programmable by authorities, while users could set conditional payments. Critics note that technical standards, wallet rules and future amendments can expand what “conditional” means without a new political debate.

03 The numbers that matter

Holding limit still undecided

Technical discussions have examined caps around €3,000–€4,000, but the final number is not law. A low cap preserves bank deposits; a high cap increases disintermediation risk; an automatic waterfall preserves convenience while weakening separation.

Why sovereignty is part of the technical debate

The ECB argues that 13 euro-area countries rely entirely on non-European companies for in-store card payments. A public rail could reduce dependency — but it could also make every merchant, wallet and user dependent on one EU infrastructure stack.

Financial privacy & resilience

A cash-first protection manual

Three tiers matched to three threat models: payment surveillance, infrastructure failure and monetary discretion. The goal is not to reject every digital tool, but to preserve exit options.

Level A

For ordinary users who want less data exposure without changing habits dramatically.

Keep a cash buffer

Use banknotes for small, frequent or sensitive purchases. Cash remains the only widely accepted payment rail that does not require an account, an app or a network.

Separate spending rails

Do not put all monthly spending on one card, one app or one wallet. Diversification reduces single-point surveillance and outage risk.

Level B

For users comfortable with self-custody, local finance networks and privacy tooling.

Diversify monetary networks

Explore mutual-credit systems, local currencies, producer cooperatives and community exchange networks where they are legal and transparent.

Audit payment permissions

Review bank and wallet permissions regularly. Disable unnecessary data sharing, marketing profiles and automatic top-ups you do not fully understand.

Preserve offline capability

Keep cash, offline payment methods and local merchant relationships that work during network failures or account freezes.

Level C

For households treating monetary sovereignty as part of resilience planning.

Cash-first budgeting

Structure essential expenses around physical cash and locally anchored providers. This is slower, but it reduces dependence on remote account infrastructure.

Self-custody where appropriate

For those who understand the risks, self-custody crypto or bearer assets can diversify counterparty risk — not because they are “better money”, but because they escape a single issuer.

Legal and operational readiness

Document accounts, beneficiaries, offline assets and dispute routes. Monetary resilience includes paperwork as much as technology.
